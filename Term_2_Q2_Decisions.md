# Term 2 (Q2) decisions

Marketplace simulation, test-market quarter. Q1 set the foundation: Speed first, Mountain second; brands Forge Aero and Forge Sprint (Speed), Forge Summit and Forge Ridge (Mountain); stores in New York and Bangalore; two printers, about 1,040 bikes per quarter.

Q2 is for testing the four bikes, setting price and advertising, and checking cash before expanding. Willingness-to-pay is a benchmark, not a price customers will accept. “About 470 bikes per store” from Q1 is not a confirmed sales cap.

## Pricing and sales priority

Sell all four brands. No rebates.

| Brand | Price | Priority |
|---|---:|---|
| Forge Aero | $1,449 | 1 |
| Forge Summit | $1,249 | 2 |
| Forge Sprint | $1,349 | 3 |
| Forge Ridge | $1,149 | 4 |

Aero is $51 under Speed’s $1,500 willingness-to-pay. Summit is $46 under Mountain’s $1,295. A $100 gap inside each segment gives a reason to consider the cheaper model. Priority follows the segment order: Speed first, then Mountain, then the lower-priced alternative in each.

Skip rebates. The position is premium. Rebates can look like unwanted inventory to less price-sensitive buyers, pull demand forward, and look like a price increase when they end. Starting at zero also gives a clean baseline for Q3.

Ridge costs more to produce than Summit ($5 in the early estimate; $577 vs $571 in the 770-unit simulation) while selling for $100 less, so its margin is the weakest. Keep it, but at the lowest priority, until customers show they want that configuration.

Unit cost depends on total production, not production of one brand. At about 100 bikes total, these prices would sit under production cost. At the 770-unit plan they do not. Simulated price minus production cost: Aero $771, Sprint $713, Summit $678, Ridge $572. That spread still has to cover shipping, stores, staff, and advertising.

## Production

**Operating capacity: 13 bikes per day.** That is 13 × 65 = **845** bikes this quarter, about 75 above the 770 forecast (about 10%). The minimum that covers 770 is about 11.9 per day. Twelve per day is only 780, a 10-bike cushion, which is thin for a first test when a stockout also hurts next quarter’s demand. Fixed capacity with two printers tops out at 16 per day (1,040).

Thirteen is not a printer count and not a promise to sell 845. Unused capacity is paid for. At the base forecast that idle cost is $18,919. A later comparison of 12 versus 13, both with overtime authorized, is still worth running; 13 is the setting that was simulated.

**Overtime authorization: 4 hours per day.** Authorization is a ceiling, not a schedule. The system uses overtime only after demand passes operating capacity, and only as many hours as it needs, up to 4. Pay for those hours is double time.

| If quarterly demand is | What happens |
|---|---|
| 845 or less | No overtime |
| 846–1,268 | Partial or full overtime |
| Above 1,268 | Production stops; stockouts remain |

At 770, the simulation produced 770, with 0 stockouts and 0.00 overtime hours. The $157.97 figure is average labor cost per bike only if all 4 overtime hours are used. With no overtime, average labor is $118.48. It is labor cost, not full production cost.

**Buy 1 printer, $240,000, for Q3.** Total printers become 3. Fixed capacity next quarter becomes 24 per day, 1,560 per quarter. Against the 1,600 forecast that is 40 short, which a small amount of overtime can cover.

| Printers added | Q3 capacity | Versus 1,600 |
|---|---:|---|
| 0 | 1,040 (1,560 even with 50% overtime) | Short 560 without overtime |
| 1 | 1,560 | Short 40; overtime covers it |
| 2 | 2,080 | 480 extra, and another $240,000 |

Do not buy the second printer because a forecast is 40 bikes over 1,560. Q3 operating capacity and overtime must be set again; do not leave the daily rate at 13.

## Cash

| Operating cash flow | +$145,374 | −$219,666 |

Base-case bridge: begin at $172,750, operating cash flow +$145,374, printer −$240,000, new equity +$500,000, end at $578,124. The equity, not operating profit, is why the quarter looks funded. Half demand falls to $213,084, which is $86,916 under the buffer, because store, ad, and staff costs stay while idle-capacity cost jumps to $116,037.

**Withdraw $150,000 from the 3-month certificate. Add $0.**

Q1 put in $450,000. The certificate does not move into the cash account by itself, so a low-cash warning while a balance remains is expected, not an emergency loan. Leave $300,000 on deposit. Quarterly interest falls to $4,500, which is $2,250 less.

Half demand then sits about $61,000 above the buffer. The cost of that insurance is $2,250 of interest. Enter the withdrawal on the certificate page and reload the cash forecast. Do not type it onto the statement by hand.
